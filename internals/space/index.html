Spaces#

Ghost spaces#

gridspace(Ωₕ; ghosts = :boundary) (or ghosts = (:xmin, :ymax) for named facets) returns a GhostGridSpace: a grid space whose degrees of freedom are the physical nodes of Ωₕ and the ghost values beyond the named facets. It holds a ScalarGridSpace on the padded mesh (a GhostMesh, see Mesh), reached by padded_space, and a closure (E, d), reached by closure, with u_pad = E u + d. Every form on a ghost space is assembled on the padded space, with the engines, patterns and replays of a plain space, and reduced afterwards.

DOF order#

The degrees of freedom are the physical nodes in the mesh's linear order, then the ghosts in the numbering of the ghost mesh, whose absent points are never degrees of freedom. The first entries of a vector of the space are therefore the values at the physical nodes, as on gridspace(Ωₕ).

Weights#

Per axis the weight of a ghost node and of a ghost cell is 0, so a form has no weight in its ghost rows and only the reduction below gives the ghosts a role. The space tutorial gives the weights and why they are physical-only.

The closure and the reduction#

On gridspace(Ωₕ; ghosts) the closure is the selection of the degrees of freedom among the padded points. E is N_pad × ndofs with one entry 1 per column, and d = 0. On the result of constrained the closure comes from the relations. E expresses each eliminated ghost through the physical nodes, and d carries the relations' data at the eliminated ghosts. Assembly fills A_pad on the padded space (on a traction facet of a composite, by the facet rule below) and returns \(E_v^T A_{\mathrm{pad}} E_u\); the load of a linear form is \(E_v^T (F_{\mathrm{pad}} - A_{\mathrm{pad}}\, d_u)\), so a space with \(d \neq 0\) needs the bilinear form, and assemble(l) alone throws an ArgumentError. The reduction is a sparse map from the stored values of A_pad to those of the reduced matrix, computed once with the form, so a warm assemble! allocates nothing. It is symmetric whenever A_pad and the two closures are.

dirichlet and symmetrize replace rows of the reduced system when its unknowns are the physical nodes in mesh order (every ghost eliminated, no node fixed by a Dirichlet relation). A Dirichlet condition is also available as the relation :F => u => g of constrained, which removes the facet's nodes from the unknowns and moves their data into d. Both routes agree on the solution.

The corner rule#

A mixed difference at a 2D corner node, or at a 3D edge node, of a ghost mesh would read an absent padded point: the tap to the facet ghost along one axis re-evaluates the inner difference there, whose tap along the other axis lands outside along both. At such a node the stencil engine uses the inward one-sided difference along both axes, D₊ at a first node and D₋ at a last, which reads physical nodes only and is second order. The rule applies to a first difference of first differences across two axes, with constant or grid-function scales, and it distributes over sums and differences of such terms.

The rule is structural: which stencil a corner node uses depends on the form, not on the values of its coefficients, so the discretisation is continuous in the data. A coefficient that is zero at the ghost tap still gets the one-sided row.

Reading an absent point is otherwise refused. A product on a ghost space asks, at every node where its weight is not zero, whether either operand reads a point the padded grid lacks, and throws an ArgumentError naming the term when one does: an average, jump or shift of a cross difference, a difference around a second difference, and deeper nestings. Reads a zero weight or a zero scale multiplies are not reads. Under a facet product, a read that leaves the padded grid (past the one ghost layer, or across a facet without ghosts) is refused too. A volume product's operand at the edge of the padded grid truncates, as on a plain mesh, and that is allowed.

The rule applies in forms only; the runtime operators are eager (see the operators tutorial). Rₕ and avgₕ refuse on a ghost space with an ArgumentError (avgₕ would average over cells past the physical facet), except that Rₕ(U, f) works on the trial space of polygon_spaces; the support table lists what else refuses.

Relations#

constrained(Wₕ, u -> (:F => operator => data, ...)) calls the lambda once with the trial placeholder of the padded space. The facet operators ∂ₙ, ∂ₙₙ and jumpₙ are builder-only: each holds one operator per (axis, side) and inner_Γ expands it into one product per incident facet with that facet's weight, so a corner node gets one value per facet and no assembly node is added. A relation of a scalar space takes its facet's component. Its rows on the facet nodes are assembled by forms, inner_Γ(relation, v; markers = (F,)), and solved for the ghosts: one entry per ghost, or a 2×2 block per 2D corner where two conormal relations read each other's ghost. Each block is solved by its own LU, and E and d stay sparse; no dense factorisation of the whole closure is formed. Ghosted facets without a relation keep their ghosts as unknowns.

On a composite C₁ × C₂, constrained(V, u -> (...)) constrains the composite as a whole, and a relation may read several components, as the traction of elasticity does. Component i of it is ∂ₙ(u(1); K = Kᵢ₁) + ∂ₙ(u(2); K = Kᵢ₂), with Kᵢⱼ a constant matrix. The relation is split by the component it reads, each piece assembled by its own inner_Γ and tested against v(i) of the ghost it is attached to. A facet whose relation reads several components carries one ghost relation per component, written unkeyed. The rows of all of them at a node, or at a corner, are solved together as one block, so component 1's relation may read component 2's ghost. A Dirichlet relation names its component, u(i) => g, and a displacement facet's ghosts are closed by ∂ₙₙ(u(i)) => 0. The rows are solved once on the padded composite: the padded space is padded(C₁) × padded(C₂), and the unknowns are component 1's then component 2's, each in the scalar order. The closure (E, d) is therefore general, not blockdiag, wherever a relation couples components; with relations of one component each it equals constrained(C₁, r₁) × constrained(C₂, r₂). The result stores its own (E, d), which closure, lift and its forms read. A component view uₕ(i) of it is refused, since E couples the components, so take lift(Vₕ, parent(uₕ))(i) instead. × and ^ of it, data depending on time, the dirichlet rows and GhostVector are refused too. A corner of two traction facets uses a one-sided tangential difference along the facets, since the two shear relations there are one equation.

On a facet whose ghost relation reads several components, a traction, the mixed differences of the form read no ghost of that facet. The mixed differences in the left argument of a product, the argument whose stencil gives the matrix columns, extrapolate the function to the ghost from its values at the three nodes inward along the normal, to second order on non-uniform spacing. Second differences and the relations still read the ghosts of the closure, and E and d are unchanged, so the relations hold on lifted values exactly as before. The shear-closed ghosts make the reduced matrix of the collocated form acquire an eigenvalue with negative real part, measured for a traction on one facet of a uniform grid (docs/notes/internals/ghost_points.md), once the cells are wider across the facet than along it. A composite whose relations each read one component is not affected.

Grid functions with ghosts#

A grid function on a ghost space has two layouts, one for time loops and one for forms.

A GhostVector is the layout of a time loop: the physical values stay the solver's own contiguous Vector, and the ghosts sit in a facet buffer in the order of the ghost mesh. It carries the mesh with ghosts. The runtime operators then know the padded spacings and the ghosted facets. A padded VectorElement is the layout of forms: lift(Cₕ, u) returns E u + d on the padded space, ghosts filled and absent points 0, and assembly already works on the padded mesh. The padded layout costs a copy in and out of about two stencil applications, which is why a time loop keeps the facet buffer. The DOF order above is the same for both, the padded layout appending the absent points as a suffix that is dropped.

update_ghosts!(v, Cₕ) fills the ghost buffer from the closure. The ghost update is precomputed when the space is built: the closure's rows for the ghosts that d and E determine are stored once, so the update is one sparse product plus the data, with no allocation on the serial and Polyester policies. With time-dependent relation data the data are affine in the unknown, d(t) = L g(t) with L fixed at construction, so update_ghosts!(v, Cₕ, t) and lift(Cₕ, u, t) evaluate g(t) at the ghosts' relations and apply L, and semidiscretize differentiates d(t) exactly for the time derivative of the load. A space with time-dependent data refuses the calls without t.

A runtime operator on a GhostVector returns the physical values as a plain Vector, with no ghosts: it reads the ghosts of its argument and truncates, as the operators do on a plain space. Composing operators on the result is that truncation, so the corner rule, which lives in the stencil engine, is never applied to a runtime composition. Mixed differences at a ghost corner stay form-only.

The reduction Eᵀ A_pad E, the load Eᵀ(F_pad − A_pad d) and lift run in bands. The reduction and the load are grouped by destination entry: each band owns a set of output entries and sums their contributions in the serial order, so CpuSerial, CpuThreaded and CpuPolyester all give the sums of the serial run, bitwise. The ! calls, lift!, the pair assemble!(A, F, a, l) and update_ghosts! allocate nothing on CpuSerial and CpuPolyester; on CpuThreaded each band pass costs the task spawn of the plain space's own call and nothing that grows with the grid.

Polygon spaces#

polygon_spaces builds (U, W) as two GhostGridSpaces over the mesh itself. Each exterior diagonal neighbour p = k + (sx, sy) of an interior node k takes the relation (3.2) of the paper, u_p = u_{k+(sx,0)} + u_{k+(0,sy)} - u_k. At a V-notch, an exterior node read by two interior nodes has two different values and so no row of the extension; the reduction substitutes the value of each reading equation, as the paper does.

The spaces of a level-set marker (:D => levelset(φ), Meshes) are the same call on the polygon inscribed in the curve: g is read at the polygon's vertices, the boundary nodes. The curve is orthogonally convex, so no line meets it four times near a notch and no exterior node has two readers; the V-notch rows below do not arise there.

Bramble.InnerProductType

source 
InnerProductType

Abstract type for selecting which discrete inner product formula to use.

Different inner product types correspond to different weight distributions on the grid, used in various finite difference schemes and stability analyses. The choice of inner product affects energy estimates and numerical stability properties.

Subtypes

  • Innerh: Standard \(L^2\) inner product using cell measures (volumes)

  • Innerplus: Modified inner product using staggered grid spacings

Background

In finite difference methods, different inner products arise naturally from:

  • Summation-by-parts (SBP) operators

  • Energy method stability analysis

  • Discrete integration formulas

The standard inner product (Innerh) uses cell volumes as weights, while the modified inner products (Innerplus) use combinations of forward/backward spacings, appearing in discrete energy estimates for difference operators.

Usage

using Bramble: inner₊ₓ
# Compute standard L² inner product
result = innerₕ(uₕ, vₕ)  # Uses Innerh() internally

# Compute modified inner product in x-direction
result = inner₊ₓ(uₕ, vₕ)  # Uses Innerplus() internally

See also: Innerh, Innerplus, innerₕ, inner₊ₓ

Bramble.Innerh

source 
Innerh <: InnerProductType

Selector for the standard discrete \(L^2\) inner product weighted by cell measures.

The weights are the volumes (lengths in 1D, areas in 2D, volumes in 3D) of grid cells, denoted \(|\square_k|\). This is the most common inner product for finite difference methods and corresponds to the trapezoid rule for integration on non-uniform grids.

Mathematical form

For a 2D grid:

\[(u_h, v_h)_h = \sum_{i,j} |\square_{i,j}| u_h(x_i, y_j) v_h(x_i, y_j)\]

where \(|\square_{i,j}|\) is the area of the cell centered at \((x_i, y_j)\).

Examples

# Compute L² inner product
result = innerₕ(uₕ, vₕ)  # Uses Innerh() internally

# Compute L² norm
norm_value = normₕ(uₕ)  # Equivalent to sqrt(innerₕ(uₕ, uₕ))

See also: InnerProductType, Innerplus, innerₕ, normₕ

Bramble.Innerplus

source 
Innerplus <: InnerProductType

Selector for modified discrete \(L^2\) inner products using staggered grid spacings.

These inner products use a combination of backward spacings \(h_i = x_i - x_{i-1}\) (zero at the first node) and cell widths \(h_{i+1/2}\), appearing naturally in energy estimates for finite difference operators. Different spatial directions may have different weight formulas.

The modified inner products are used for:

  • Proving discrete energy stability

  • Analyzing discrete conservation properties

  • Constructing stable finite difference schemes

Mathematical form

For a 2D grid in the x-direction:

\[(u_h, v_h)_{+x} = \sum_{i,j} h_{x,i} h_{y,j+1/2} u_h(x_i, y_j) v_h(x_i, y_j)\]

Examples

using Bramble: inner₊ₓ, inner₊ᵧ
# These functions use Innerplus internally
result_x = inner₊ₓ(uₕ, vₕ)  # Modified inner product, x-direction
result_y = inner₊ᵧ(uₕ, vₕ)  # Modified inner product, y-direction

See also: InnerProductType, Innerh, inner₊ₓ, weights

Bramble.SeparableWeights

source 
SeparableWeights{D, T, VT}(factors::NTuple{D, VT}, dims::NTuple{D, Int})

A lazy, separable weight vector over a D-dimensional grid: entry I (linear or CartesianIndex{D}) is \(\prod_{d=1}^D\) factors[d][I[d]], computed on every access rather than stored once for the whole grid.

Every family SpaceWeights offers is one of these (gpena/Bramble.jl#115, #234; O(nD) storage rather than O(n^D)): innerh and each entry of innerplus are built once, when the space is constructed, from the per-axis aligned and cellfactor factors, and stored on SpaceWeights – so reading them costs no more than the field access plus whatever the caller's own indexing does. weights(Wₕ, Val(S)) for |S| ≥ 2 builds a fresh one on every call instead, from those same two tuples, since nothing yet asks for the same larger set twice in a hot loop.

A linear getindex converts to a CartesianIndex first (one division per axis); a caller that already holds the CartesianIndex – an assembly loop over local_stencil, for instance – should use it directly and skip that cost. src/space/inner_product.jl's _dot/_dot_masked do this for every weight family alike, and so does src/operators/inner.jl's compute_weight for InnerH, InnerPlus{Dim} and InnerPlusSet (|S| ≥ 2): a symbolic innerₕ/inner₊ₓ/etc. term inside a form reads its weight by the CartesianIndex the assembly loop already holds, with no division per point (gpena/Bramble.jl#428).

On device-backed factors (gpena/Bramble.jl#310), getindex raises an error naming host_weights rather than letting GPUArraysCore throw its own scalar-indexing message: Array(w) and host_weights(w) are the two bulk-transfer escapes, each pulling the D per-axis factor vectors to the host once and evaluating the tensor product (or keeping it lazy, for host_weights) there instead.

Fields

  • factors::NTuple{D, VT}: the per-axis vectors multiplied together at each index.

  • dims::NTuple{D, Int}: the grid shape, npoints(Ωₕ, Tuple).

See also: weights, SpaceWeights.

Bramble.SpaceWeights

source 
SpaceWeights(innerh::SeparableWeights, innerplus::NTuple{D}, aligned::NTuple{D, VT}, cellfactor::NTuple{D, VT}, built_version::Int)
SpaceWeights{D, T, VT}(innerh, innerplus, aligned::NTuple{D, VT}, cellfactor::NTuple{D, VT}, built_version::Int)

Holds the diagonal weight vectors for a grid space's discrete inner products, both the standard \(L^2\) weights and the staggered ones, precomputed once rather than recomputed on every call.

Fields

  • innerh::SeparableWeights{D, T, VT}: weight for the standard discrete \(L^2\) inner product (:innerₕ), based on cell measures (\(|\square_k|\)). Built once, straight from cellfactor (no axis is in S = ()), and stored here rather than recomputed on every access – it is a lazy per-axis product rather than a full-grid vector (gpena/Bramble.jl#115, #234), so weights(Wₕ, Innerh()) costs the field read plus whatever the caller's own indexing does; see SeparableWeights.

  • innerplus::NTuple{D, SeparableWeights{D, T, VT}}: one such lazy weight per spatial direction, for the modified, staggered inner products (:inner₊ₓ, :inner₊ᵧ, etc.).

  • aligned::NTuple{D, VT}: per-axis factor \(h_d(i)\) (length npoints(Ωₕ, d), not the full grid) – the same values innerplus uses on the axis aligned with its own difference direction. Kept so weights(Wₕ, Val(S)) can build any staggered set from O(D) numbers instead of a fresh O(n^D) vector (gpena/Bramble.jl#115, #234).

  • cellfactor::NTuple{D, VT}: per-axis factor \(h_d(i+1/2)\) (also length npoints(Ωₕ, d)). This is innerh's own per-axis cell measure – innerh's factors tuple is cellfactor, not a copy of it – and doubles as every staggered direction's transverse factor: the two coincide for any axis with more than one point (both read the submesh's own cached half-spacings), so one vector serves both roles rather than two.

Bramble.shift — Union{Tuple{i}, Tuple{SHIFT_DIM}, Tuple{Bramble.AbstractMeshType, Val{SHIFT_DIM}, Val{i}}} where {SHIFT_DIM, i}

source 
shift(Ωₕ::AbstractMeshType, ::Val{SHIFT_DIM}, ::Val{i})

Returns the matrix that shifts a grid function by i points along direction SHIFT_DIM, in the backend's matrix_type, over the flattened degrees of freedom of Ωₕ.

Arguments

  • SHIFT_DIM: the direction to shift along, 1 for \(x\), 2 for \(y\), 3 for \(z\).

  • i: how far to shift. 1 is the superdiagonal, -1 the subdiagonal, 0 the identity. The stencil is truncated at the boundary rather than wrapped, so the matrix has n - |i| nonzeros per direction rather than n.

Tensor-product structure

A mesh is a tensor product of its one-dimensional meshes, and its degrees of freedom are flattened in column-major order, so a shift along one direction is the identity in every other direction. Writing \(E_k\) for the identity of size \(n_k\) and \(S_k(i)\) for the one-dimensional shift by i, the operator is a Kronecker product with \(S\) in one slot:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{along } x: &\quad E_z \otimes E_y \otimes S_x(i) \\ \text{along } y: &\quad E_z \otimes S_y(i) \otimes E_x \\ \text{along } z: &\quad S_z(i) \otimes E_y \otimes E_x \end{aligned}\]

_recursive_shift builds exactly this, recursing from the outermost dimension inwards and placing \(S\) when it reaches SHIFT_DIM. The per-direction forms it generalises, each of which the test suite checks against shift:

# 1D
shift(Ωₕ, Val(1), Val(i))  ==  _Eye(be, nₓ, Val(i))

# 2D, on an nₓ × n_y grid
shift(Ωₕ, Val(1), Val(i))  ==  backend_eye(be, n_y) ⊗ _Eye(be, nₓ, Val(i))
shift(Ωₕ, Val(2), Val(i))  ==  _Eye(be, n_y, Val(i)) ⊗ backend_eye(be, nₓ)

# 3D, on an nₓ × n_y × n_z grid
shift(Ωₕ, Val(3), Val(i))  ==  _Eye(be, n_z, Val(i)) ⊗ backend_eye(be, nₓ * n_y)

i == 0 short-circuits to the identity of the whole grid without building any Kronecker product.

It builds the S₊/S₋ matrix forms and the Kronecker oracle kronecker_operator_matrix; the operator families' own matrices come from stencil_matrix.

See also: ⊗, diff₋ₓ.

Bramble.centered_difference! — Tuple{Bramble.VectorElement{var"#s709", T} where {var"#s709"<:Bramble.ScalarGridSpace, T}, Bramble.VectorElement{var"#s708", T} where {var"#s708"<:Bramble.ScalarGridSpace, T}, Val}

source 
centered_difference(uₕ::VectorElement, dim_val::Val)

The centered difference of uₕ along dim_val:

\[\textrm{Dc}(\textrm{u}_h)(i) = \frac{\textrm{u}_h(x_{i+1}) - \textrm{u}_h(x_{i-1})}{h_i + h_{i+1}}\]

Reached through Dcₓ and its siblings, and takes a mesh, a grid space or a grid function as the other difference families do.

The denominator is \(x_{i+1} - x_{i-1}\), so the operator reproduces the derivative of an affine function exactly on any grid, uniform or not. Both the first and the last point lack a neighbour on one side, so both are truncated to zero.

See also: star_spacings, D₋ₓ, D₊ₓ.

Bramble.cross_weighted_difference! — Tuple{Bramble.VectorElement{var"#s709", T} where {var"#s709"<:Bramble.ScalarGridSpace, T}, Bramble.VectorElement{var"#s708", T} where {var"#s708"<:Bramble.ScalarGridSpace, T}, Val}

source 
cross_weighted_difference(uₕ::VectorElement, dim_val::Val)

The cross-weighted centered difference of uₕ along dim_val:

\[\overset{\times}{\textrm{D}}_{h}(\textrm{u}_h)(i) = \frac{h_i}{h_i + h_{i+1}}\, \textrm{D}_{-}\textrm{u}_h(x_{i+1}) + \frac{h_{i+1}}{h_i + h_{i+1}}\, \textrm{D}_{-}\textrm{u}_h(x_i)\]

Reached through D̽ₓ and its siblings, and takes a mesh, a grid space or a grid function as the other difference families do.

It is the same two one-sided differences Dcₓ combines, weighted by the opposite spacings. That is the combination which cancels the leading truncation term on a non-uniform grid, so this is second order where Dcₓ is first, and the two coincide when the spacing is constant.

The first and the last point each lack a neighbour on one side, but unlike Dcₓ neither is truncated: each collapses to the one-sided difference its near side still defines, D₊ₓ(uₕ) at the first point and D₋ₓ(uₕ) at the last.

See also: Dcₓ, D₋ₓ, D₊ₓ.

Bramble.cross_weighted_difference — Union{Tuple{DIM}, Tuple{Bramble.AbstractMeshType, Val{DIM}}} where DIM

source 
cross_weighted_difference(Ωₕ::AbstractMeshType, dim_val::Val)

The cross-weighted centered difference along dim_val, as a sparse matrix.

A three-point stencil in the interior, but neither end row is empty: with no neighbour on the far side, row 1 agrees with D₊ₓ(Ωₕ, dim_val) and row n with D₋ₓ(Ωₕ, dim_val), each under its own diagonal weight alongside the interior cross-weighting. The mesh still needs at least three points along the direction.

Bramble.InterpolationNode

source 
InterpolationNode{D, S, OpType} <: LazyOp{D}

The symbolic interpolation operator: inner_op lives on src_space, and this node evaluates it at points of whatever mesh the assembly is walking.

Side is TrialSide or TestSide, taken from the leaf πₕ wrapped. It decides which leaf _bind_interp_spaces stamps into src_space, whether the stencil names AbsoluteColumns or AbsoluteRows, and which leaf's grid the sweep walks: always the other one, the side that stays native.

src_space is nothing until assembly binds it. πₕ(op) cannot name the space itself: on a composite space the leaf a term interpolates from is only resolved block by block, so _bind_interp_spaces stamps that leaf in at that point, once per block, and the S === Nothing node exists only between construction and that binding.

Distinct from the source wrapper πₕ(uₕ), which carries a grid function's values. This node carries no values; it carries the map, and its stencil names degrees of freedom of the space it interpolates from.

outside is one of :error, :clamp or :extrapolate – never a fill value – and is stored as an isbits OutsidePolicy enum rather than the Symbol given to πₕ, so the node holds no Symbol. A fill value is refused since this node's stencil is a linear map (weighted trial columns), and a constant independent of the trial unknowns cannot be written that way; see interpolation_matrix's docstring for the same restriction.

Which side of a bilinear term an InterpolationNode sits on.

A trial-side interpolation names absolute columns and leaves the rows to the walked mesh; a test-side one names absolute rows and leaves the columns to it. The side is a type parameter rather than a field so the node stays a singleton, and so every walk that reads it folds away at compile time.

Bramble._surface_sum — Union{Tuple{D}, Tuple{Bramble.DeviceLocality, Bramble.GpuPolicy, Bramble.AbstractMeshType{D}, Any, AbstractVector, AbstractVector}} where D

source 
_surface_sum(::DeviceLocality, ::GpuPolicy, Ωₕ, mask, u, v) -> Number

Device counterpart of _surface_sum above: a canonical face (or a union of them) is a set of at most 2D axis-aligned (D-1)-dimensional slices of the grid, so the CPU walk over every grid point – reading half_spacings and u/v one scalar at a time – becomes, per active face, one broadcasted reduction over that slice.

u and v are reshaped to the grid's own dims (a view, not a copy). For each axis d and side (min/max) the mask marks, selectdim takes the boundary slice directly – no mask array is built or walked here, unlike _dot_masked's device path, since a coordinate face is already a contiguous slice rather than an arbitrary marked set. The transverse surface measure for that slice is built once from host_half_spacings (never read on the device), then copied onto u's own device in one transfer via _transverse_weight_device; the reduction itself is sum(conj.(su) .* sv .* w), no @kernel.

Faces are summed independently rather than through one shared mask, exactly as the host _surface_weight_dir recursion does: a corner point on two active faces receives both faces' contributions, which is the intended, non-double-counting definition documented above _face_mask.

Bramble._transverse_weight_device — Union{Tuple{D}, Tuple{d}, Tuple{Bramble.AbstractMeshType{D}, Any, Val{d}, Val{D}}} where {d, D}

source 
_transverse_weight_device(Ωₕ::AbstractMeshType{D}, u, ::Val{d}, ::Val{D}) -> AbstractArray

The (D-1)-dimensional transverse surface measure for the face normal to axis d, as a device array matching u's own backend.

Built the way _separable_weights_full builds the full weight tensor – one reshape per transverse axis so broadcasting multiplies them out to the full transverse shape – except every factor here comes from host_half_spacings rather than a SeparableWeights factor that is already device-resident, since this is a fresh geometric quantity with nothing cached to read. The host tensor is built once, from vectors of length O(n^{1/D}), then copied onto u's device in a single transfer – never read back scalar by scalar the way half_spacing would on the host path.
